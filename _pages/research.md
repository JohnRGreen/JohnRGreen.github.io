      <h4 id="research-jmp">Beliefs and Preferences in Community College</h4>
      <p><a href="https://github.com/JohnRGreen/CCSubjectiveBeliefs">Read working paper →</a></p>
      <p class="research-paper__abstract">Despite high labor market returns, the majority of community college students never earn a 2-year degree, and only a fraction transfer to 4-year programs. I collect novel survey data on the expected choices of community college students, and on their beliefs about earnings, tuition costs, and dropout risks. Beliefs appear to deviate from rational expectations: respondents are optimistic about dropout risk, but pessimistic about 4-year tuition costs, with ambiguous net effect on transfer. An information experiment demonstrates that students are responsive to new information, suggesting the potential for correcting biases. In part to quantify the importance of such corrections, I use the data to estimate a dynamic model of community college student choices. Students' plans are most sensitive to the four-year dropout risk, moderately responsive to earnings beliefs, but largely inelastic with respect to expected tuition costs. An exercise in correcting beliefs shifts individuals away from transfer and towards community college dropout, with improved sorting generating significant welfare benefits. I use the model to test the plausibility of various hypotheses for why students' planned outcomes may differ from the high dropout rate that characterize the community college landscape. Results suggests that learning about 4-year dropout risk may play a meaningful role, but is insufficient to close the gap. I demonstrate that "academic mismatch", where students discover they are a worse match for higher education than they believe at the start, is a plausible mechanism for these two phenomena.</p>

      <h4 id="research-pivot">Labor Supply, Social Welfare, and the Great MTR Pivot in U.S. Tax and Transfer Programs</h4>
      <p class="research-paper__authors">With <a href="https://www.emmakalish.com/home">Emma Kalish</a> and Robert Moffitt</p>
    <p class="research-paper__abstract">Over the past 50 years, marginal tax rates facing those in the low income population who participate in transfer programs have fallen at low earnings levels and correspondingly risen at higher income levels. We call this development the Great MTR Pivot, because it has rotated the marginal tax rate schedule in a counterclockwise direction.  We make three contributions to this literature.  First, we provide a comprehensive accounting of all major transfer and tax credit programs over the period 1983-2019. We calculate benefits and cumulative marginal tax rates for individuals participating in different combinations of programs and demonstrate how they have changed over time.  Second, we construct a static model of labor supply and program participation which takes account for the full range of tax and transfer programs. We then calibrate the model to a sample of less-educated single mothers constructed from the Current Population Survey, fitting the parameters of the model to match reduced form estimates of the causal impact of each program appearing in the literature and the take-up rate of each program from administrative data and reports to allow for nonparticipation of eligibles. The estimated model provides a unified accounting of all tax and transfer programs, their takeup rates, and their combined effects on labor supply.  We use the calibrated model to estimate the impact of the Pivot from 1983 to 2019 on labor supply, program participation, and social welfare, using a variety of social welfare metrics.  Third, we construct the counterfactual impacts of alternative revenue-neutral changes in the MTR schedule and of a few hypothetical optimal schedules.  Our findings show that the Pivot, which theoretically has ambiguous net effects on labor supply, has increased both labor supply and welfare in the low-income population studied. While increasing labor supply and social welfare, the Pivot has at the same time increased income and social welfare inequality.</p>

      <h4 id="research-me">Measurement Error in Subjective Beliefs</h4>
    <p class="research-paper__abstract">Beginning with Dominitz and Manski (1996), a large literature has grown which directly elicits subjective expectations. Although it is well known that such data suffer from significant measurement error, the majority of work in this area does not address this. In this paper, I show how an information experiment can be used to flexibly identify measurement error. Control group measurements before and after the placebo treatment provide multiple measurements for the same latent belief, allowing for classical measurement error. By assuming that similar "types" of beliefs (such as full-time earnings at ages 25 and 30) share characteristics of the measurement system, I am able to allow for some forms of non-classical measurement error. I demonstrate the importance of addressing this error with two empirical exercises. First, I show how controlling for measurement error is important in assessing the impacts of the information experiment itself. Second, I show how, conditional on an assumption of stability across waves, an information experiment administered in cross-section allows for more accurate study of belief updating across time.</p>

      <h4 id="research-combo">Can Workforce Training Soften the Scarring Effect?</h4>
      <p class="research-paper__authors">With <a href="https://www.wonsikko.com/">Wonsik Ko</a> and Su Wang</p>
    <p class="research-paper__abstract">Workforce training programs are an affordable and accessible way for workers to develop new skills and improve their labor market prospects. This may be particularly important for workers who lose their employment: job loss has been shown to have a long-lasting scarring effect on future earnings and employment, but workers who are able to build their human capital after becoming unemployed may be able to avoid some of these impacts. Existing evaluations of workforce training programs generally use administrative data, and as a result suffer from an important shortcoming of such datasets: they typically contain total earnings, which are the product of labor supply (hours) and a wage. This is a serious limitation, since the impact of a training program depends upon which margin it operates; as we demonstrate, a positive impact on earnings is not even sufficient to show that an intervention is welfare improving. Recognizing that earnings (the product of hours and wages) places bounds on the range of each component, we utilize machine learning tools and auxiliary data to estimate the joint distribution of hours and wages conditional on earnings and a sparse set of shared covariates. We demonstrate that our method performs well by presenting three out-of-sample (and out-of-dataset) validation exercises replicating previous results. Finally, we apply this method to administrative data from Maryland to study the impact of workforce training programs on the wages and hours of workers who lose their jobs in the COVID-19 recession, overcoming a limitation of previous such analyses.</p>
