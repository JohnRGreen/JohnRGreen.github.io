      <h4 id="research-jmp">Beliefs and Preferences in Community College</h4>
      <p class="research-paper__abstract">Despite high labor market returns, the majority of community college students never earn a 2-year degree, and only a fraction transfer to 4-year programs. I collect novel survey data on the expected choices of community college students, and on their beliefs about earnings, tuition costs, and dropout risks. Beliefs appear to deviate from rational expectations: respondents are \textit{optimistic} about dropout risk, but \textit{pessimistic} about 4-year tuition costs, with ambiguous net effect on transfer. An information experiment demonstrates that students are responsive to new information, suggesting the potential for correcting biases. In part to quantify the importance of such corrections, I use the data to estimate a dynamic model of community college student choices. Students' plans are most sensitive to the four-year dropout risk, moderately responsive to earnings beliefs, but largely inelastic with respect to expected tuition costs. An exercise in correcting beliefs shifts individuals away from transfer and towards community college dropout, with improved sorting generating significant welfare benefits. I use the model to test the plausibility of various hypotheses for why students' planned outcomes may differ from the high dropout rate that characterize the community college landscape. Results suggests that learning about 4-year dropout risk may play a meaningful role, but is insufficient to close the gap. I demonstrate that ``academic mismatch,'' where students discover they are a worse match for higher education than they believe at the start, is a plausible mechanism for these two phenomena.</p>

Draft abstract for the Great MTR Pivot paper, retained from the previous page until it is ready to publish:
Over the past 40 years, marginal tax rates facing those in the low income population who participate in transfer programs have fallen at low earnings levels and correspondingly risen at higher income levels, a development we term the Great MTR Pivot  because it has pivoted the marginal tax rate schedule in a counterclockwise direction.  We make three contributions to this literature.  First, we provide a more comprehensive accounting of all major transfer and tax credit programs over the period 1983-2019 and calculate new cumulative marginal tax rates for individuals participating in different combinations of programs and how they have changed over time.  Second, we calibrate the parameters of a static structural labor supply model to a sample of less-educated single mothers constructed from the Current Population Survey, fitting the parameters of the model to the labor supply elasticities and program takeup rates estimated for each of the major U.S. safety net programs, resulting in a model which has a unified accounting of all programs and their combined effects on labor supply.  Third, we use the calibrated model to estimate the impact of the Pivot on labor supply and on social welfare over the period, the counterfactual impacts of alternative revenue-neutral changes in the MTR schedule, and a few hypothetical optimal schedules.  We find that the Pivot, which theoretically has ambiguous net effects on labor supply, to have increased it as well as increasing aggregate welfare in the low-income population studied, while at the same time having increased inequality in income and welfare.
