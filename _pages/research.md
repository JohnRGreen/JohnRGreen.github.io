### Works in progress

1. **Subjective Beliefs and the Choices of Community College Students** (job market paper)

    Despite high labor market returns, the majority of community college students never earn a 2-year degree, and only a fraction transfer to 4-year programs. I analyze these phenomena using novel survey data on the beliefs of community college students about earnings, tuition costs, and dropout risks. Beliefs appear to deviate from rational expectations: respondents are optimistic about dropout risk, but pessimistic about 4-year tuition costs. However, students are responsive to new information, suggesting the potential for correcting biases and improving outcomes. To investigate, I use the data to estimate a dynamic model of community college student choices. The results show a small elasticity of transfer with respect to tuition costs and a moderate elasticity with respect to dropout beliefs, with earnings falling in between. Correcting beliefs yields small aggregate changes, but improved sorting generates significant positive welfare effects. A policy which encourages transfer *with* a 2-year degree faces a targeting problem, but this issue is mitigated by information provision, pointing to the importance of accounting for beliefs when constructing financial aid policies.

    
2. **Labor Supply, Social Welfare, and the Great MTR Pivot in U.S. Tax and Transfer Programs** (with Emma Kalish and Robert Moffitt)

{% comment %}
    Over the past 40 years, marginal tax rates facing those in the low income population who participate in transfer programs have fallen at low earnings levels and correspondingly risen at higher income levels, a development we term the Great MTR Pivot  because it has pivoted the marginal tax rate schedule in a counterclockwise direction.  We make three contributions to this literature.  First, we provide a more comprehensive accounting of all major transfer and tax credit programs over the period 1983-2019 and calculate new cumulative marginal tax rates for individuals participating in different combinations of programs and how they have changed over time.  Second, we calibrate the parameters of a static structural labor supply model to a sample of less-educated single mothers constructed from the Current Population Survey, fitting the parameters of the model to the labor supply elasticities and program takeup rates estimated for each of the major U.S. safety net programs, resulting in a model which has a unified accounting of all programs and their combined effects on labor supply.  Third, we use the calibrated model to estimate the impact of the Pivot on labor supply and on social welfare over the period, the counterfactual impacts of alternative revenue-neutral changes in the MTR schedule, and a few hypothetical optimal schedules.  We find that the Pivot, which theoretically has ambiguous net effects on labor supply, to have increased it as well as increasing aggregate welfare in the low-income population studied, while at the same time having increased inequality in income and welfare.
